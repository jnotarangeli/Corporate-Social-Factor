The 100 best companies to work for 

In a tough year these companies tried to do right by their employees How we pick the 100 best

We choose the 100 Best from an ever-growing pool of candidates that ask to be on the list. This year 279 companies applied, up from 234 last year. Two-thirds of the score is based on how randomly selected employees respond to the Great Place to Work Trust Index, a survey instrument measuring the quality of workplace culture. This year 44,848 employees filled out surveys, sending them directly to the Great Place to Work Institute, the consulting firm that created the survey. More than 18,000 employees also provided written comments. The remainder of the score is based on our evaluation of company responses to the Institute's Culture Audit.

Followers of this list may notice the absence of a couple of 100 Best stalwarts. The disappearance of Enron comes as no surprise. Big mergers tend to have dramatic repercussions in the workplace, so Hewlett-Packard, on the list from the start, had to sit out because of its planned merger with Compaq. (This "sitting out" period applies to mergers adding more than 25% to a company's work force.) What about Southwest Airlines, in the top five for the past four years? Well, it chose not to compete this year. We hope it rejoins the fray in the future. Companies in business for at least seven years with more than 500 employees have been eligible (we're raising the minimum to 1,000 employees for next year's list). If you think your company ought to be ranked among the 100 Best, please write us at 100best@ greatplacetowork.com.

--Robert Levering and Milton Moskowitz

The Anatomy of a Great Workplace

No. 1 Edward Jones

Employees: 27,092

It would be crass to credit Edward Jones' ascension on our list to money. But what the heck: Partners, who make an initial investment in the brokerage firm, earn an average 24% return each year. All employees benefit from profit sharing, usually amounting to about 5% of their regular incomes.

No. 4 TDIndustries

Employees: 1,368

At TDIndustries in Dallas, employees are eligible for a 401(k) plan after just 90 days and can get up to $7,000 for referring a new hire. They get generous medical insurance benefits and sick leave too, although TD, which installs infrastructure in buildings, has an injury rate 58% lower than the industry average.

No. 57 Pfizer

Employees: 89,936

Pfizer pushes health care in the workplace as well as the marketplace. It has fitness facilities at 11 sites, many offering yoga, prenatal fitness, and nutritional counseling. On-site doctors provide diabetes screenings and flu shots, free of charge. Employees on one campus can escape into the Solitude Room.

No. 90 Marriott

Employees: 141,599

Hotel work--making beds, serving food--sounds like a dead end, but many Marriott employees see it as a beginning. Nearly 20% of its managers come from the hourly ranks. The chain offers career counseling and management training, as well as English classes for its immigrant employees.

Big company, small feel

Jones has more than 8,000 branches worldwide, many with just one or two investment specialists. Employees like the small offices that offer both coziness and independence.

Bucks and babies

Brokers earn commissions, but new moms like Karin Behrens-Bouvier don't have to sacrifice money for maternity leave. Jones pays 75% of a broker's average commissions for the previous half year for six weeks after delivery.

Workers first

Despite a tough year in the brokerage industry, people like Connie Fuller, branch office administrator in Exeter, N.H., could count on their jobs. Jones didn't lay off a single employee.

Dead-end deliverance

TD trains new hires in electrical engineering, plumbing, and the like. Tom Jade (left) ditched a drudge job as a customer service rep and now installs air-conditioning systems. TD trained Paco Casales to become a sheet-metal designer.

Just one of the guys

CEO Jack Lowe (a 37-year veteran) has no reserved parking space, answers his own phone, and works in an eight-by-11-foot cubicle. He couldn't put on airs even if he wanted to: The employees own the company.

Why leave?

Nearly 14% of the employees have worked at TD for more than ten years. The longevity award goes to project manager John Gerhards, who joined during Eisenhower's presidency.

Empty chairs, full pockets

Pfizer laid off 2,351 employees after buying Warner-Lambert, but offered generous severance: 21 weeks' pay for everyone, plus three weeks' pay for each year of service; benefits for a year; and a $5,000 retraining allowance.

Cheap eats

At the subsidized cafeteria, workers can start the day at the $1.75 omelet bar and end it by ordering a take-home dinner for the whole family. It's delivered to their offices by 4:30.

Well-trained work force

Tom Dion, a convention services manager, got his training at a company learning and development site in Rye, N.Y. When not in class, trainees can squeeze in fun at the golf course or tennis courts.

Subsidized sundries

Employees stock up at the Pfizer drugstore, where stuff sells for a song. A 1.5-liter bottle of Listerine goes for $2.14, and those who overdid it at the cafeteria can pick up 12 rolls of Rolaids for 39 cents.

From dishes to decisions

Remy Perez started at Marriott 15 years ago dishing food. Thanks to her talent and the support of in-house training, she's now an assistant culinary manager at this Dallas-Marriott Quorum.

Women rule

Marriott offers coaching, education, and conferences for women--in fact, half its managers are women. In 1974, Charlotte Allo Collier began as an hourly associate typing menus; now she's a senior vice president overseeing 20,000 employees.

--Alynda Wheat
